Case study · Spring 2026

Touch-free medical glove and sanitiser dispenser

A battery-powered station that keeps gloves sealed away until a hand is detected, dispenses sanitiser, tracks how many glove boxes are left, and sounds an alarm if the hatch is left open. It runs on a Raspberry Pi Pico and was built for under £100.

The problem

Most clinics keep disposable gloves in open, wall-mounted boxes. Every glove still in the box is exposed to droplets, coughs, and whoever reached in last. Srigley et al. (2014) found bacteria on and around gloves that were still in their boxes. The one product we found that closes the box, the AeroGlove, is designed for food-service gloves and is expensive.

Glove dispensers and sanitiser dispensers also exist only as separate products. Our proposal was a low-cost, motion-activated station that uses the gloves and sanitiser a clinic already buys, so staff never touch the box before gloving up.

Generating and choosing concepts

We used a morphological chart, listing options for each function (detection, glove dispensing, sanitiser delivery, structure), then scored complete concepts in a weighted decision matrix.

FunctionOptions considered
DetectionButton · IR sensor · ultrasonic sensor
Glove dispensingManual pull · roller · servo grabber
Sanitiser deliveryPump bottle · servo press · peristaltic pump
StructureWall-mounted · desktop · enclosed box

We ruled out every "touch it yourself" option (button, manual pull, pump bottle) straight away, because each one brings back the cross-contamination we were trying to remove. For the dispensing mechanism we compared a suction hose (slow, and dependent on how gloves sit in the box), a printer-style roller (needs a fixed glove orientation and grips unevenly), and a motorised pinch-and-lower bracket, which works with any glove box.

The criteria were hygiene (30%), ease of use (20%), technical feasibility (20%), cost (15%), and innovation (15%). The winning concept scored 5/5 on hygiene and ease of use but only 3/5 on feasibility. That turned out to matter.

Iteration 1: pinch-and-lower

The plan: two servos on brackets pinch a single glove with custom ridged arms. Four DC gear motors spin 10-inch threaded poles that the brackets ride on, lowering them. When the IR sensor sees a hand, the servos pinch, the poles lower the brackets, and the servos release the glove into a tray.

Most of my time went into the threaded poles. I printed and tested 24 versions between 12 and 27 March: single-piece poles, separate male and female sections, and paired sets designed to join. I also went through 5 servo arms and 5 servo mounts.

Why I scrapped it

  • The printer couldn't print the part

    The rods needed to be 10 inches long. Our printer couldn't print threaded rods longer than about 7 inches, so later versions split each pole into male and female sections.

  • The threads came out rough

    Print supports couldn't be fully removed from the threads, so the brackets bound instead of riding smoothly.

  • The motors weren't strong enough

    Even when a pole turned, the small gear motors couldn't reliably lift the brackets against that friction.

  • Back to brainstorming

    After more than 50 hours, I accepted that the mechanism was too ambitious and was getting in the way of the actual goal. We kept the enclosure idea, dropped the moving bracket, and focused on protecting the gloves instead of handing them out one at a time.

The lesson I took: test mechanical feasibility before sinking serious time into a design.

Final design: four functions, fewer moving parts

The redesign is a laser-cut acrylic enclosure (9 × 4.5 × 10 in) that holds up to three glove boxes. Boxes load from the top and an empty one comes out through a side door. A 3D-printed housing on top holds the Pico, breadboard, LCD, and batteries.

The alarm wasn't in the original plan. A fourth subsystem, a self-cleaning tray, wasn't finished in time, so I built the open-hatch alarm in its place. A fourth function that worked beat a fourth one that didn't.

Fabrication

I modelled every part I was responsible for in Autodesk Inventor, exported STLs for printing, and used Inventor's sheet tools to produce DXFs for the laser cutter. Prints ran on a Prusa CORE One (PLA, 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layers, 30% infill).

Print times were a real schedule constraint, and our team retrospective named them as one of the things that slowed us down. Short test prints, like the 9-minute thread coupon, let me check a fit before committing hours to a full part.

Results

  • All of the final functions ran together on battery power in the finished prototype.
  • The glove hatch, inventory display, and open-hatch alarm all worked as designed.
  • Purchased consumables (gloves and sanitiser gel) came to £12.87. Electronics and stock came from the lab, well within the £100 cap.
  • The final design protects the gloves rather than dispensing them one at a time. That was a deliberate trade-off for reliability.

Reflection

What I'd do differently. I'd test the riskiest mechanism first, with the cheapest possible prototype, before designing everything around it. I'd also learn more about material and process limits up front: how long a part can be printed, and how supports behave on threads.

What I'd improve next. A moulded enclosure with proper hinges; a solenoid or linear actuator instead of the servo, for a better hatch seal; a single Pico instead of two; and a smaller footprint. Before any real clinical use, it would also need validation and a fail-safe way to reach the gloves.

Ethics. The report included an ethics analysis using consequentialist, deontological, and virtue-ethics lenses. The conclusion was that the device should be presented as a hygiene aid that supports good practice, not a replacement for it.
